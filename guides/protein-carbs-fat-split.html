Protein, Carbs and Fat: Finding Your Split

How to divide your daily calories between protein, carbohydrate and fat, and how the split changes with your goal.

Once you know how many calories to eat, the next question is how to divide them between the three macronutrients: protein, carbohydrate and fat. There is no single perfect split, but there are sensible ranges, and the right one depends on your goal, your training, and what you enjoy eating. This guide explains what each nutrient does and how to choose a split you can live with.

What each nutrient does

Protein builds and repairs muscle, keeps you full, and costs more energy to digest than the other nutrients. Carbohydrate is the body's preferred fuel for hard exercise and the brain's main fuel. Fat is essential for hormones, vitamin absorption and long-term energy, and it makes food satisfying. Alcohol is a fourth source of calories, at 7 per gram, but it provides no useful nutrients. Protein and carbohydrate each provide 4 calories per gram, and fat provides 9, which is why fatty foods are so energy-dense.

A sensible starting split

A common starting point for general health is about 20 to 30 percent of calories from protein, 40 to 50 percent from carbohydrate, and 25 to 35 percent from fat. For a 2,000-calorie day, that means roughly 100 to 150 grams of protein, 200 to 250 grams of carbohydrate, and 55 to 78 grams of fat. These are ranges, not rules, and the exact split matters less than hitting your calorie target and eating enough protein. If you prefer a lower-carbohydrate or higher-fat style, that can work too, as long as the total calories and protein are right.

Protein for weight loss and muscle

Protein deserves special attention, because it is the nutrient most people under-eat when dieting. A higher protein intake, around 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight a day for active people, helps preserve muscle during weight loss and supports muscle gain when training. It also keeps you fuller than the same calories from carbohydrate or fat, which makes a deficit easier to live with. Spread protein across meals rather than eating it all at dinner, because the body uses it better that way.

Adjusting the split for your goal

For weight loss, keep protein high and let carbohydrate and fat fall to fit the calorie target. For muscle gain, keep protein high and add carbohydrate, because it fuels training and is easy to eat in larger amounts. For endurance training, carbohydrate matters most, because it refills the glycogen stores that fuel long sessions. For a sedentary person, the exact split matters least of all, and the priority is simply eating a varied diet within the calorie target. In every case, the total calories decide whether you lose, gain or maintain weight.

Making it practical

You do not need to hit exact numbers every day. Plan your protein first: a palm-sized portion at each meal is a good rule. Fill the rest of the plate with vegetables and a carbohydrate source such as rice, bread or potatoes, and include a little fat from olive oil, nuts or dairy. Track for a week with an app to see where you actually land, then adjust the biggest gap. Over time, the split becomes automatic, and you can stop counting and simply eat in the pattern you have learned.

A day at a 30-40-30 split

Take a 2,000-calorie day split as 30 percent protein, 40 percent carbohydrate and 30 percent fat. That means 150 grams of protein, 200 grams of carbohydrate and 67 grams of fat. A day that hits it might look like this: eggs and toast for breakfast, chicken and rice for lunch, yoghurt and fruit as a snack, and fish with potatoes and vegetables for dinner. The protein comes mostly from the eggs, chicken, yoghurt and fish, the carbohydrate from the toast, rice and potatoes, and the fat from olive oil, nuts and dairy. You do not need to hit the numbers exactly every day; within 5 percent is fine, and the pattern matters more than the precision. Track for a week to see where you actually land, then adjust the biggest gap.

Tip: Protein first is the simplest rule: a palm-sized portion at each meal, aiming for roughly 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight a day if you train.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good macro split?

A common starting point is 20–30% protein, 40–50% carbohydrate and 25–35% fat. The exact split matters less than total calories and enough protein.

How much protein do I need?

Around 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight a day for active people, and less for sedentary people. Spread it across meals.

Do carbs make you fat?

No. Excess calories make you gain weight, whatever the source. Carbohydrate is the main fuel for hard exercise.
