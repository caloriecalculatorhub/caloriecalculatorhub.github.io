What Is BMR? Basal Metabolic Rate Explained

What basal metabolic rate means, how it is calculated, and why it is the foundation of every calorie estimate.

Basal metabolic rate, or BMR, is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest just to stay alive: breathing, circulating blood, keeping your organs working and maintaining body temperature. It accounts for most of the calories most people burn in a day, typically 60 to 75 percent. Understanding BMR is the key to understanding every calorie calculator, because total daily needs are simply BMR multiplied by an activity factor.

What BMR actually measures

BMR is measured under strict conditions: lying down, fully rested, awake but not moving, in a comfortable temperature, after fasting for about 12 hours. In that state, your body still burns a surprising amount of energy. The brain alone uses roughly a fifth of resting energy, and the liver, heart and kidneys use large shares too. Muscle tissue burns more energy at rest than fat tissue, which is why two people of the same weight can have different BMRs. A calculator cannot measure your BMR directly; it estimates it from your age, sex, weight and height.

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation

Most modern calculators use the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which researchers consider the most accurate simple formula for most people. For men it is 10 times weight in kilograms, plus 6.25 times height in centimetres, minus 5 times age in years, plus 5. For women the same formula ends with minus 161 instead of plus 5, reflecting the average difference in body composition. The result is an estimate in calories per day. Older formulas such as Harris-Benedict give similar but slightly higher numbers, and the differences are usually small.

Worked example

Take a 30-year-old woman who weighs 65 kg and is 165 cm tall. Her BMR is 10 times 65, which is 650, plus 6.25 times 165, which is 1,031, minus 5 times 30, which is 150, minus 161. That gives 1,370 calories a day at rest. For a 30-year-old man of the same weight and height, the calculation is 650 plus 1,031 minus 150 plus 5, which gives 1,536 calories. The difference comes from the formula's sex adjustment, and it reflects average differences in muscle mass, not a rule about individuals.

What raises or lowers BMR

Muscle is the biggest factor you can influence: more muscle means a higher BMR, because muscle burns more energy at rest than fat. That is why strength training raises BMR over time, while crash dieting can lower it, because rapid weight loss often loses muscle as well as fat. Age lowers BMR gradually, by roughly 1 to 2 percent per decade after early adulthood, mainly because muscle mass declines. Illness, fever and pregnancy raise BMR, while some medicines and thyroid conditions can shift it in either direction.

Why BMR is not your daily target

Eating exactly your BMR would leave you short, because you also burn energy moving, working, exercising and digesting food. Your total daily needs are BMR multiplied by an activity factor, from 1.2 for a sedentary life to 1.9 for a very physical one. For the woman in the worked example, a moderate activity factor of 1.55 turns a BMR of 1,370 into daily needs of about 2,124 calories. BMR is the foundation, not the finished number, and the activity factor is what turns it into a useful daily target.

BMR in everyday terms

Think of BMR as the cost of keeping the lights on. Even if you lay in bed all day, your body still spends energy: your brain uses about a fifth of resting energy, your liver and kidneys use large shares, and your muscles burn energy just by existing. That is why BMR is most of your daily burn, and why it is the foundation of every calorie estimate. A useful comparison is a house: BMR is the standing charge for heating, lighting and water, and activity is everything extra, from cooking to running the washing machine. You cannot reduce the standing charge much, but you can raise it slowly by building muscle, and you can raise the total a lot by moving more. Understanding the split makes the numbers feel less abstract and the plan easier to follow.

Tip: BMR is roughly 60 to 75 percent of the calories most people burn in a day — the rest comes from everything you do, including walking, working and digesting food.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a normal BMR?

It depends on size, age and sex. Many adults fall between about 1,200 and 2,000 calories a day at rest. Larger and younger people tend to have higher BMRs.

Does muscle really raise BMR?

Yes, but modestly. Muscle burns more energy at rest than fat, so building muscle raises BMR over time. The effect is real but small compared with activity.

Can I measure my BMR exactly?

Only in a laboratory, by measuring oxygen use at rest. Calculators estimate it with a formula, which is accurate enough for planning.
